Trump's 10% Credit Card Interest Rate Cap: What It Means for Your Wallet

February 17, 2026 • 10 min read • By Credit Gamer Team
Advertisement

Imagine paying no more than 10% interest on your credit card. For millions of Americans carrying credit card debt, this sounds like a dream come true. In recent policy discussions, former President Trump has floated the idea of capping credit card interest rates at 10%—a dramatic reduction from today's average rates that often exceed 20%.

But before you celebrate, it's important to understand what this proposal really means, how it might affect you, and why the banking industry is pushing back hard against it. Like most financial policies, the devil is in the details.

📊 The Current State of Credit Card Interest Rates

To understand the significance of a 10% cap, let's look at where we are today. As of early 2026, the average credit card APR (Annual Percentage Rate) in the United States hovers around 22-24% for new offers. For existing accounts, the average is closer to 20%. These rates have climbed steadily as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to combat inflation.

Here's how current rates break down by credit tier:

  • Excellent Credit (750+): 16-18% APR
  • Good Credit (670-749): 20-22% APR
  • Fair Credit (580-669): 24-27% APR
  • Poor Credit (Below 580): 28-36% APR (if approved at all)

Store credit cards and retail cards often charge even higher rates, sometimes exceeding 30%. For someone carrying a $5,000 balance at 25% APR, they're paying over $100 per month in interest alone.

💡 What the 10% Cap Proposal Entails

The proposal being discussed would federally cap credit card interest rates at 10%—similar to how some states have usury laws that limit interest rates. This isn't a new concept; credit card rates were capped in the past, and some countries still maintain strict limits on consumer lending rates.

📈 Historical Context In 1980, Congress passed legislation that effectively deregulated interest rates, allowing credit card companies to charge rates based on market conditions rather than state usury limits. This led to the modern credit card industry we know today.

The 10% figure isn't arbitrary—it aligns with traditional usury laws and represents a return to more regulated lending practices. Proponents argue that credit card companies have enjoyed record profits while consumers struggle with mounting debt.

✅ Potential Benefits for Consumers

If implemented, a 10% interest rate cap could provide significant relief for millions of Americans:

1. Dramatic Interest Savings

Let's do the math. On a $5,000 balance:

  • At 25% APR: You pay $1,250/year in interest
  • At 10% APR: You pay $500/year in interest
  • Annual savings: $750

For families carrying $10,000+ in credit card debt, the savings could exceed $1,500 annually—money that could go toward actually paying down principal rather than lining bank pockets.

2. Faster Debt Payoff

Lower interest rates mean more of your monthly payment goes toward the principal balance. A $200 monthly payment on a $5,000 balance at 25% APR would take about 3 years to pay off. At 10% APR, you'd be debt-free in just over 2 years—and save hundreds in interest.

3. Reduced Financial Stress

High-interest debt is a leading cause of financial anxiety and bankruptcy. Lower rates could help millions avoid the debt spiral that starts with a small balance and grows into an unmanageable burden.

⚠️ Potential Downsides and Unintended Consequences

While lower rates sound appealing, economists and industry experts warn of several potential negative outcomes:

1. Tighter Credit Availability

Banks use interest rates to offset the risk of lending to consumers with lower credit scores. If rates are capped at 10%, banks may simply stop lending to higher-risk borrowers rather than accept lower returns. This could make credit cards inaccessible to the very people who need them to build credit.

⚠️ The Subprime Problem Consumers with credit scores below 620 might find themselves unable to get any credit card at all. Currently, high APRs allow banks to serve these customers while pricing in the risk. A rate cap could eliminate this market entirely.

2. Death of Rewards Programs

Those lucrative cashback offers, travel rewards, and signup bonuses? They're largely funded by interest payments from cardholders who carry balances. If interest revenue drops by 50-60%, banks will almost certainly slash rewards programs to maintain profitability.

Your 2% cashback card might become a 0.5% card—or disappear entirely. Premium travel cards with $500+ signup bonuses could become a thing of the past.

3. Higher Fees and New Charges

Banks are creative when it comes to revenue. If they can't make money on interest, they'll find other ways:

  • Higher annual fees (currently $0-100, could rise to $200+)
  • Increased balance transfer fees
  • New "account maintenance" fees
  • Foreign transaction fees on all cards
  • Elimination of grace periods

4. Reduced Competition and Innovation

Smaller banks and fintech companies might exit the credit card market entirely if they can't compete with larger institutions that have economies of scale. This reduced competition could lead to fewer card options and worse customer service.

🎯 What Should You Do Now?

Whether this proposal becomes law or not, here are smart moves to make:

1. Pay Down High-Interest Debt

Don't wait for potential rate relief. If you're carrying balances at 20%+, prioritize paying them down now. Consider balance transfer cards with 0% introductory APRs, or look into debt consolidation loans.

2. Lock in Good Rewards Cards

If you have excellent credit, now might be the time to apply for premium rewards cards. If the proposal passes, these offers could disappear. Just be sure you can pay off balances in full each month.

3. Build Your Credit Score

Regardless of what happens with interest rates, having excellent credit will always benefit you. Focus on:

  • Paying all bills on time (35% of your score)
  • Keeping credit utilization under 10% (30% of your score)
  • Not closing old accounts (helps with credit history length)

4. Consider Alternative Credit Options

If credit cards become harder to obtain, having alternative credit sources matters:

  • Credit union cards (often have lower rates)
  • Personal lines of credit
  • Buy-now-pay-later services (use responsibly)
  • Secured credit cards for building credit

🔮 The Bottom Line

A 10% credit card interest rate cap is a double-edged sword. It could save billions for consumers carrying debt, but it might also reduce credit access, eliminate rewards programs, and introduce new fees.

The proposal faces significant hurdles—banking industry opposition, legal challenges, and economic uncertainty all stand in its way. Even if it moves forward, expect a long legislative process and potential compromises.

Your best strategy? Don't rely on policy changes to fix your finances. Focus on what you can control: building excellent credit, paying down debt aggressively, and using credit cards as tools rather than crutches.

The most powerful interest rate is 0%—the rate you pay when you pay your balance in full every month. That's a strategy no politician can change.
