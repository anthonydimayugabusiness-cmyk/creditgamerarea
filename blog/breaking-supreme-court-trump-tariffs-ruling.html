Supreme Court Strikes Down Trump's Tariffs in Major Ruling

February 20, 2026 • 6 min read • Politics & Markets
⏱️ Last Updated: 11:11 PM ET, February 20, 2026
C

In a stunning 6-3 decision, the U.S. Supreme Court struck down President Donald Trump's sweeping global tariffs on Friday, dealing a major blow to a cornerstone of his economic agenda and sending shockwaves through financial markets.

The ruling invalidates the "reciprocal" tariffs Trump imposed on nearly every country using emergency powers, marking the first major defeat for the administration at the nation's highest court despite a series of recent wins on the court's emergency docket.

📋 Key Facts at a Glance

  • Vote: 6-3 decision, with Justices Alito, Thomas, and Kavanaugh dissenting
  • What was struck down: "Reciprocal" tariffs imposed under 1977 emergency powers law (IEEPA)
  • Economic impact: ~$3 trillion over next decade (per CBO)
  • Revenue collected: Over $133 billion in tariffs under the emergency law
  • What remains: Some tariffs under different authorities still in place

What the Court Ruled

The Supreme Court ruled that the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) — the law Trump used to justify his tariffs — does not authorize the President to impose tariffs.

The decision centers on Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs announced in April 2025, which imposed sweeping duties on most countries to address trade deficits that Trump declared a national emergency. The tariffs came after earlier duties on Canada, China, and Mexico, ostensibly to combat drug trafficking.

"The law that undergirds those import duties does not authorize the President to impose tariffs."

— Supreme Court Majority Opinion

Who Challenged the Tariffs

The legal battle crossed political lines, with challengers including:

  • 12 states (mostly Democratic-leaning)
  • Small businesses selling plumbing supplies, educational toys, women's cycling apparel, and more
  • Libertarian and pro-business groups typically aligned with the GOP
  • Major retailers like Costco, which have lined up to demand refunds

The challengers argued that IEEPA doesn't even mention tariffs and that Trump's use of it fails several legal tests — including one that doomed President Biden's $500 billion student loan forgiveness program.

Financial markets welcomed the ruling, with stocks jumping on the news as investors priced in reduced trade uncertainty and lower consumer prices.

What This Means for Consumers

The ruling could provide relief for American consumers who have faced higher prices on everything from electronics to clothing due to the tariffs. The Congressional Budget Office estimated Trump's tariff agenda would cost the economy approximately $3 trillion over the next decade.

💡 What Could Get Cheaper

  • Electronics and semiconductors from China
  • Clothing and textiles
  • Auto parts and vehicles
  • Household goods and appliances
  • Raw materials for construction and manufacturing

What Happens Next

The ruling doesn't mean the end of Trump tariffs entirely. Here's what to watch:

🔮 Next Steps to Watch

  1. Refund demands: Companies like Costco are already lining up to demand refunds on tariffs paid under the invalidated law
  2. Alternative authorities: The administration may try to impose similar tariffs using Section 232 (national security) or other trade laws
  3. Congressional action: Lawmakers could attempt to clarify or expand presidential tariff authority
  4. Trade negotiations: The ruling may push the administration toward traditional bilateral trade deals rather than unilateral tariffs
  5. Legal appeals: While the Supreme Court has ruled, implementation details may face additional challenges

The Broader Implications

This ruling represents a significant check on executive power and reaffirms Congress's constitutional authority over trade policy. The Constitution explicitly gives Congress — not the President — the power to levy tariffs and regulate commerce with foreign nations.

For Trump, it's a rare loss at the Supreme Court, which he helped shape with three conservative appointments during his first term. The court had previously allowed the administration to proceed with various controversial policies through its emergency docket.

Polling has shown tariffs aren't broadly popular with the public, particularly amid ongoing concerns about affordability and inflation. The legal opposition crossing traditional political lines — with libertarian and business groups joining Democratic states — reflects the widespread economic impact of the trade policies.
