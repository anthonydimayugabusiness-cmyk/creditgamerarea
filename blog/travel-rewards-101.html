Travel Rewards 101: Beginner's Guide to Free Flights & Hotels

Everything you need to know to start earning free travel with credit card points and miles

📅 Feb 23, 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read ✈️ Beginner Guide

Imagine booking a round-trip flight to Hawaii or a week-long hotel stay without spending a dime. Sounds too good to be true? It's not. Welcome to the world of travel rewards — where your everyday spending becomes your ticket to free travel.

If you're new to travel rewards, the learning curve can feel steep. Points, miles, transfer partners, award charts — it's a lot to digest. But here's the good news: you don't need to be an expert to start earning free travel. In this guide, we'll break down everything you need to know to get started, from choosing your first credit card to booking your first free flight.

What Are Travel Rewards?

Travel rewards are points or miles you earn through credit card spending, airline loyalty programs, and hotel loyalty programs. These rewards can be redeemed for free flights, hotel stays, car rentals, and even cash back.

There are three main types of travel rewards:

  • Airline Miles: Earned through flying and airline co-branded credit cards. Redeemable for flights on that airline and its partners.
  • Hotel Points: Earned through hotel stays and hotel co-branded credit cards. Redeemable for free nights at that hotel chain.
  • Flexible Points: Earned through bank rewards programs (Chase, Amex, Citi, Capital One). Can be transferred to multiple airlines and hotels or used to book travel directly.

💡 Key Insight

Flexible points are the most valuable for beginners because they give you options. You're not locked into one airline or hotel chain — you can choose the best redemption for your specific trip.

How Travel Rewards Work: The Basics

Here's the simple formula: spend money on a rewards credit card → earn points → redeem points for free travel. But to maximize your rewards, you need to understand a few key concepts.

1. Sign-Up Bonuses

The fastest way to earn a lot of points quickly is through credit card sign-up bonuses. These offers typically give you 50,000 to 100,000+ points after you spend a certain amount (usually $3,000-$5,000) within the first 3-6 months.

One sign-up bonus can be worth $500-$1,500 in free travel — more than you'd earn from years of regular spending.

2. Category Bonuses

Most rewards cards earn bonus points in specific categories:

  • Travel: 2x-5x points on flights, hotels, and car rentals
  • Dining: 2x-4x points at restaurants
  • Groceries: 2x-6x points at supermarkets
  • Gas: 2x-3x points at gas stations
  • Everything else: 1x-1.5x points on all other purchases

3. Point Values

Not all points are created equal. Here's a rough guide to point values:

1¢ Cash Back Baseline
1.25¢ Chase Travel Portal
2¢+ Transfer Partners
5¢+ Premium Cabin Redemptions

Choosing Your First Travel Rewards Card

If you're just starting out, you want a card that's beginner-friendly, has a solid sign-up bonus, and earns flexible points. Here are our top recommendations:

Best Starter Card: Chase Sapphire Preferred

The Chase Sapphire Preferred is the gold standard for beginners. Here's why:

  • 60,000-point sign-up bonus (worth $750 in travel)
  • 2x points on travel and dining
  • 25% bonus when booking through Chase Travel
  • Transfer partners including United, Southwest, Hyatt, and Marriott
  • $95 annual fee (reasonable for the value)

Best No-Annual-Fee Option: Chase Freedom Unlimited

If you're not ready to pay an annual fee, the Chase Freedom Unlimited is a great choice:

  • 1.5x points on everything (with no cap)
  • 3x points on dining and drugstores
  • $0 annual fee
  • Points can be combined with Chase Sapphire cards for more value

Best for Frequent Travelers: Capital One Venture

If you want simplicity, the Capital One Venture card is hard to beat:

  • 2x miles on everything — no categories to track
  • 75,000-mile sign-up bonus
  • Easy redemption: Erase any travel purchase with miles
  • $95 annual fee (waived first year)

The 5/24 Rule: What You Need to Know

Before you start applying for cards, you need to understand Chase's 5/24 rule. This unofficial rule states that Chase will not approve you for most of their cards if you've opened 5 or more credit cards (from any bank) in the past 24 months.

Why this matters: Chase has some of the best travel rewards cards and transfer partners. If you're just starting out, prioritize Chase cards before getting cards from other banks.

🚀 Your First 90 Days: Action Plan

  1. Check your credit score — You typically need a score of 670+ to qualify for travel rewards cards.
  2. Count your recent card applications — If you've opened fewer than 5 cards in the past 24 months, start with Chase.
  3. Apply for the Chase Sapphire Preferred — This is the best beginner travel card with a generous sign-up bonus.
  4. Meet the minimum spend — Plan your spending to hit the $4,000 requirement in 3 months. Don't spend more than you normally would.
  5. Set up automatic payments — Always pay your balance in full to avoid interest charges.
  6. Learn the transfer partners — Familiarize yourself with Chase's airline and hotel partners.

How to Redeem Your Points

Once you've earned points, you have several redemption options:

1. Book Through the Bank's Travel Portal

The simplest option. Log into your credit card account and book flights, hotels, or car rentals directly.

Pros: Easy, no blackout dates, earn airline miles on flights
Cons: Fixed value (usually 1-1.25 cents per point)

2. Transfer to Airline Partners

Move your points to an airline's frequent flyer program, then book award flights.

Pros: Best value (often 2+ cents per point), access to partner airlines
Cons: More complex, limited award availability, transfers are irreversible

3. Transfer to Hotel Partners

Move your points to a hotel loyalty program for free nights.

Pros: Can be great value (especially Hyatt), no blackout dates at many properties
Cons: Point values vary widely by hotel

Common Mistakes to Avoid

As a beginner, it's easy to make mistakes that cost you value. Here are the most common pitfalls:

1. Carrying a Balance
Interest charges will quickly erase any rewards you earn. Always pay your balance in full.

2. Redeeming for Cash Back
Most travel points are worth more when used for travel. Cash back typically gives you only 1 cent per point.

3. Letting Points Expire
While many programs have no expiration, some do. Keep your accounts active with occasional earning or redemption.

4. Not Meeting Minimum Spend
The sign-up bonus is where the real value is. Make sure you can meet the spending requirement before applying.

5. Ignoring Annual Fees
Make sure the benefits you receive outweigh the annual fee. Many cards offer statement credits that offset the fee.

Building Your Strategy

As you get more comfortable with travel rewards, you can build a more sophisticated strategy:

Step 1: Establish Your Foundation

Start with one or two cards that earn flexible points. Learn how to use them effectively before adding more.

Step 2: Add Category Bonuses

Once you have a foundation, add cards that earn bonus points in categories where you spend the most (groceries, dining, gas).

Step 3: Explore Transfer Partners

Learn which transfer partners offer the best value for your travel goals. This is where you unlock 2+ cents per point.

Step 4: Consider Hotel and Airline Cards

If you're loyal to specific airlines or hotels, their co-branded cards can offer valuable perks like free checked bags and elite status.

The Bottom Line

Travel rewards can seem overwhelming at first, but the basics are simple: use a rewards card for your everyday spending, earn points, and redeem them for free travel. Start with a beginner-friendly card like the Chase Sapphire Preferred, pay your balance in full every month, and you'll be booking your first free trip in no time.

Remember, the goal isn't to maximize every penny — it's to make your travel dreams more affordable. Even if you only get 1.5 cents per point in value, that's still 50% more than cash back. And as you learn more, you'll unlock even greater value.
