The Art of Slow Travel: Why I Stopped Counting Countries

I spent years chasing passport stamps. Then I discovered slow travel—and it changed everything. Here's why staying longer in fewer places beats rushing through many.

February 25, 2026 • 8 min read

I used to be that traveler. The one who bragged about visiting 30 countries before turning 30. The one who planned itineraries with military precision: three days in Paris, two in Amsterdam, a quick stop in Brussels. Check, check, check.

I had the photos to prove it. Me in front of the Eiffel Tower. Me at the Anne Frank House. Me eating waffles in Brussels. What I didn't have was any real understanding of those places.

"I was collecting countries like Pokémon cards. But I wasn't actually traveling—I was just moving through places."

Everything changed when I spent a month in Lisbon on a whim. What was supposed to be a five-day stop turned into four weeks. And those four weeks taught me more about travel than the previous five years combined.

What Is Slow Travel?

Slow travel isn't about moving slowly (though you might). It's about staying longer in fewer places. It's the difference between visiting a city and living in one, even temporarily.

Instead of racing through 10 countries in two weeks, you might spend those two weeks in one city. Or one month in one neighborhood. The goal isn't to see everything—it's to see something deeply.

The Comparison: Fast vs. Slow Travel

Fast Travel (2 weeks, 5 cities) Slow Travel (2 weeks, 1 city)
Spend 2-3 days per city Spend 14 days in one place
See major tourist attractions Discover hidden neighborhoods
Eat at restaurants near tourist sites Become a regular at local spots
Take photos of famous landmarks Have conversations with locals
Feel like you're "doing" Europe Feel like you understand a place
Return home exhausted Return home transformed

Why Slow Travel Wins

1. You Actually Get to Know a Place

After a week in a city, you stop using Google Maps. You know which cafe has the best morning light. You know which bakery opens first. You know the shortcut through the park that saves five minutes.

This isn't just convenient—it's a completely different relationship with a place. You're not observing from the outside anymore. You're participating.

2. You Build Real Connections

When you're somewhere for two days, everyone you meet is transactional. The waiter. The hotel staff. The Uber driver. Quick exchanges, then gone.

Stay for a month, and you have time for real relationships. The barista remembers your order. The guy at the corner store asks about your day. You get invited to things. You become part of the community, however temporarily.

3. You Save Money (Without Trying)

Monthly Airbnb discounts. Cooking your own meals. Not paying for constant transport between cities. Knowing where the cheap eats are because you have time to find them.

Slow travel isn't about being cheap—it's about being efficient. You spend less and experience more.

4. You Can Work (If You Want)

Two days isn't enough time to settle into a work routine. Two weeks? You can find a coworking space, establish a schedule, and actually be productive.

This is how you fund extended travel without burning savings. Work for a month, explore for a month. Repeat.

5. You Avoid Travel Burnout

Fast travel is exhausting. Constant packing, constant navigating, constant decision-making. By day 10 of a rapid-fire trip, you're running on fumes.

Slow travel gives you space to breathe. Days where you do nothing. Days where you work from a cafe. Days where you just exist in a place without any agenda.

How to Start Slow Traveling

You don't need to quit your job or become a digital nomad. You just need to shift your mindset:

Start Small

Instead of planning a trip to "Europe," plan a trip to one city in Europe. Stay for a week instead of a weekend. See how it feels.

Rent an Apartment, Not a Hotel

Hotels are for sleeping. Apartments are for living. Having a kitchen changes everything. You shop at local markets. You cook with local ingredients. You live like someone who belongs there.

Work From There (Even Part-Time)

If you can work remotely even one day a week, do it from somewhere interesting. A long weekend becomes a week when you work Monday and Friday from your destination.

Say Yes to Random Invitations

When you're somewhere for two days, you can't be spontaneous. You have a schedule. When you're somewhere for two weeks, you can say yes to that random party, that day trip, that dinner invitation.

Learn Something

Take a cooking class. Learn the language. Study the history. Having a focus gives your time there purpose—and connects you with people who share that interest.

The Truth About "Seeing Everything"

Here's what nobody tells you: you can't see everything anyway. The person who visits 30 countries has 30 superficial experiences. The person who visits 5 has 5 deep ones.

And in 10 years? You won't remember the 12th cathedral you saw. You'll remember the conversation you had with a stranger over coffee. The meal someone cooked for you. The sunset you watched from your apartment window.

"The goal of travel isn't to see things. It's to be changed by what you see. And that takes time."

My Challenge to You

On your next trip, try this: cut your destinations in half and double your time in each. If you were planning four cities in two weeks, do two cities in two weeks instead.

Notice what changes. Notice how you feel. Notice what you discover when you're not rushing.

You might find, like I did, that less really is more.
